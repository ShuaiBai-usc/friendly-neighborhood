Why hydrants go dry / hillside cross-section

How to read it
Each zone's water level is set by its tank. A hydrant's pressure is how far that level sits above it: every 2.3 ft of height is 1 psi.

When a tank empties, the level drops. Once it falls below a hydrant, that hydrant is dry.

04
Mechanism

A valve that closes when the house is gone

Firefighters can't shut off thousands of water lines in the middle of a firestorm. The house has to do it for them.

  1. Step 1 / Sense

    A sensing loop circles the house

    A thin loop of pipe branches off the water service and runs around the house, with temperature sensors along it.

  2. Step 2 / Shut

    Fire breaks the loop, the valve shuts

    When heat or a collapse breaks the loop, the valve on the main service line closes and the house stops drawing water from the street.

  3. Step 3 / Report

    The utility sees it, the owner resets it

    Sensors report which homes have shut off, so the utility knows where water is being lost. After the fire, a quick-reset kit reopens the line.

Model / data / limits

How we built this

Every dot on the map is a real structure from CAL FIRE's post-fire damage inspection, and every hydrant is a real LADWP or LA County hydrant. We estimated when the fire reached each structure by georeferencing CAL FIRE's progression map, and assigned each point a pressure zone from its ground elevation.

The water model is small on purpose. Destroyed homes leak in proportion to the square root of local pressure. Firefighting draws water where the fire is. Pumps lift water to three hillside tanks and slow down as their intake pressure falls. We tuned it once so the "What happened" run matches the public record, then ran the valve scenarios with the same settings.

It is a model, not a reconstruction. The biggest unknown is how much water one destroyed house leaks; no public figure exists, so it is a calibrated assumption.
